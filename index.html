How I work

Numbers tell me what happens. People tell me why.

Many researchers lean on one side. I'm trained in both, and I choose the method that fits the decision the team has to make.

Quantitative

When the question is how much, how often, or when.

  • Controlled experiments and eye-tracking
  • Survey design, norming and validated psychometric scales
  • Statistics: descriptive statistics, t-tests, ANOVA, regression, linear mixed-effects models, cluster analysis, CFA, SEM, MaxDiff, divergence-point analysis and data visualization
  • Pre-set quality thresholds and exclusion criteria
  • Experiment programming in Python (PsychoPy)
  • Machine learning in Python (scikit-learn, PyTorch)

Qualitative

When the question is why, and what's missing.

  • Interviews with users and stakeholders
  • Competitor benchmarking
  • User personas and learning-flow design in Figma
  • Facilitating diagnostic and brainstorming workshops
  • Turning findings into value propositions and recommendations

My rule: before I collect any data, I write down what would count as a pass. If something fails, I fix the material and test again. I don't lower the bar.

Case study 01 · Experimental research

How do we predict the next word before we hear it?

M.Sc. research · European Master in Clinical Linguistics (EMCL++) · Ghent University · 2026 · Poster talk at AMLaP 2026 · Preregistered on OSF

Listeners don't wait for a word to finish. They guess what is coming. But do we predict what a word means before what it sounds like, or both at once? To answer that, I needed two things: stimuli I could trust, and a way to watch prediction as it happens.

Part 1Online surveys~100 participants · 2 rounds

Before measuring prediction: how do you build stimuli you can trust?

An eye-tracking study is only as good as its materials. If a sentence doesn't clearly point to one picture, or people call a picture by different names, the eye movements mean nothing. So I treated stimulus creation as a study of its own.

The hardest part: recruitment

I needed 96 usable native Belgian-Dutch speakers, tested in person in the lab, in a short period. I advertised online and in person, approached people directly, and kept going through rejection after rejection until the sample was complete. In total I recruited more than 100 people, and 96 met the criteria.

What we found

Sound-based prediction came first. Looks to the rhyming picture rose during the sound cue and lasted into the meaning cue, before meaning-based prediction appeared. But neither one dominated the other: the sound cue didn't delay meaning prediction (no interaction, and the same divergence point with or without it). That is precedence without competition, which fits a parallel model of prediction.

What makes it new: unlike earlier studies, the sound cues were purely phonological (repeated non-words with no meaning). So any extra looks to the rhyming picture can only come from sound-based prediction. That breaks the strict sequential model, in which listeners predict meaning first, then grammar, then sound.

What this means in a UX team

The question changes from “what do listeners predict?” to “what do users expect to happen next, and where do they look first?”. The way of working stays the same.

  • Norming stimuli before the experimentPiloting a survey or prototype before the real study
  • One clear metric, tied to time windowsDefining success metrics and the moments that matter in a flow
  • Mixed-effects models for people and itemsAnalysis that holds up when users and tasks both vary
  • Recruiting 100+ people against a deadlineRunning participant recruitment and research operations
  • Reporting a null interaction honestlyTelling a team what the data does not support

Case study 02 · Organizational research

What were employees, teachers and interns missing?

R&D / HR internship · Hamrah-e Aval Academy (MCI, Iran's first mobile operator) · Tehran · 2022–2023

The academy had two missions: accessible, high-quality education, and an internship program for recruiting talent into the company. I worked on three projects, each for a different user group, and used the method each question called for.

2AQuantitative · surveyn = 50+

Employees: where is the employee experience falling short?

Challenge
Management wanted evidence on job satisfaction, motivation and quality of work life.
What I did
Co-led the project with a colleague. We compared validated questionnaires on reliability, subscales and norms, then gave the Minnesota and Smith job satisfaction questionnaires, Hackman & Oldham's job motivation questionnaire and Walton's quality of work life questionnaire (published reliabilities α = .79–.92) to more than 50 employees. We scored responses against published norms and cut-offs, flagged items below threshold or critically below standard, and grouped them into themes.
Outcome
Presented the priority problem areas to management and facilitated a brainstorming session to turn them into interventions. Results are confidential.
Shows
Choosing validated scales · benchmarking against norms · turning data into priorities
2BQualitative · interviewsTwo-sided platform

Teachers and students: what would make online teaching work for both?

Challenge
After COVID lockdowns made online education a necessity, teachers moving online lacked support and students lacked trustworthy tutoring.
What I did
Co-led a team project. Interviewed teachers and the leaders of an existing online education platform to find what was missing for a smooth move from classroom to online teaching. Turned the findings into a business model canvas for “Moallemdan” and revised it into a second version.
Outcome
A platform concept built around trust: mentoring for new teachers, free workshops, accredited certificates and a link to the national school platform, Shad.
Shows
User and stakeholder interviews · designing for two user groups · turning needs into value propositions

2CWorkshop · diagnosisFunnel

Interns: why didn't talented people apply, or stay?

Challenge
The internship program attracted fewer applicants than expected, and many interns took jobs elsewhere afterwards.
What I did
Ran a diagnostic session and a structured brainstorm with academy staff, treating the attraction problem and the retention problem separately.
Outcome
Attraction: job fair, university partnerships, job board, informal tea talks. Retention: upward performance appraisal (staff rating their managers) and fair-treatment training for line managers.
Looking back
With more time, I'd test this with data: application and retention figures, exit interviews with former interns, and the scales from 2A.

What this means in a UX team

These were three different users, so I used three different methods. That is the daily work of a UX researcher: picking the method that fits the question.

  • Validated job-satisfaction scales and normsUsing standard UX scales (like SUS) and benchmarks instead of home-made questions
  • Interviewing teachers and platform leadersResearching both sides of a two-sided product
  • Splitting attraction from retentionSeparating an acquisition problem from a retention problem before proposing fixes
  • Presenting priorities to managementTurning findings into decisions stakeholders can act on

Side project · Inclusive design

How can a game help children with dyslexia learn to read?

M.Sc. coursework · Team lead, team of four

I led a team of four in outlining two serious games for children with dyslexia. Both drew on multisensory learning: children learn letters and sounds better when they combine what they see, hear and do. The two designs put that principle into practice differently, so the real design question was which to build.

Concept A · VR game

Closest to the theory.

  • Adds body movement to learning, to stimulate it further
  • Most in line with multisensory learning principles

Concept B · App-based game

Easiest to put in children's hands.

  • More feasible to build in practice
  • Broader accessibility, so it can reach more children

The trade-off: the design closest to the theory versus the one more children could actually use.

JavaScript · Online experiment

Can you ignore where a stimulus appears?

A Simon task I programmed in JavaScript to measure inhibitory control, a core executive function. You respond to a stimulus's color (Z for red, / for green) and ignore where it appears on screen. When the location points to the opposite key, people slow down and make more errors. That gap, the Simon effect, shows how well someone suppresses an automatic response.

Try it yourself (on a computer with a keyboard).

Python · Machine learning

Can a model tell who is speaking from one sentence?

In a team of three, I compared two speaker-recognition models (ECAPA-TDNN and ReDimNet) on 114 speakers, varying how many sentences each speaker enrolled with. The equal error rate stayed below 0.5% in every setting, even with one sentence. We also built a demo that records two voices and says whether they match. My course work also covers pitch-based gender classification (5 classifiers, best 96% accuracy), clustering (k-means, GMM, hierarchical) and CNN, LSTM and Transformer models on speech features.

Want to try it? Record two voice clips and see whether both models think they come from the same speaker. Change your tone or pitch and see how each model reacts. (Runs in Google Colab; you'll need a Google account and a microphone.)
